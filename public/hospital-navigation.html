The hospital wayfinding problem

Large Indian hospitals are hard to walk through. OPDs move, wings look alike, and signage cannot keep up. Patients and attendants stop at every desk to ask for directions — which adds stress, delays appointments, and loads front-desk staff with the same question all day.

Traditional answers are more boards, more volunteers, or a mobile app with Bluetooth beacons. Apps need downloads. Beacons need hardware, power, and maintenance. BootWay takes a different path: QR-based indoor navigation that works in the browser.

How BootWay hospital navigation works

  1. Scan a QR code at the gate, reception, or printed on a prescription. The map opens in the phone browser.
  2. Choose the destination — department, doctor, lab, ward, or room — and set the starting landmark.
  3. Follow turn-by-turn directions across floors, with lift or stairs options and distance remaining.

When a department moves, the digital map updates. Visitors get the new path immediately — without a campus-wide signage reprint.

OPD wayfinding: where most hospitals feel the pain

The busiest failure point is usually the outpatient journey — gate to registration, registration to the right OPD, then lab, pharmacy, or imaging. Attendants who have never visited before ask the same questions at every desk. A hospital navigation system should start with those high-volume paths, then expand to wards, ICUs, and specialist blocks.

Administrators planning digital wayfinding should map: (1) peak destinations by footfall, (2) languages patients actually read, (3) whether wheelchair and lift-only paths are required from day one, and (4) how often departments move. Those four inputs drive both patient experience and project cost.

QR vs beacons vs app — for Indian hospitals

Beacon-backed systems need hardware install and often a native app. Academic projects at some AIIMS campuses combine QR web maps with GPS apps. BootWay is built as a commercial vendor product for hospitals that want app-free, beacon-free indoor navigation with multilingual routes and accessible paths.

No. Visitors scan a QR code and get directions in the browser. No mobile app and no Bluetooth beacons.
