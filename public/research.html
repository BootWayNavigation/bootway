Research summary

Why patients get lost in hospitals

What published studies say about signage, patient stress, and wayfinding — and what it means for administrators planning digital indoor navigation in Indian hospitals.

Getting lost in a hospital is not a soft problem. It raises anxiety, delays care, and burns staff time answering the same directions again and again. Static signage helps, but large multi-floor campuses change faster than boards can be reprinted. The sources below are the research backing that case — with a short takeaway for hospital administrators after each finding.

1. Patients notice when signage fails them

Studies of how patients perceive hospital signage systems show that unclear or inconsistent signs increase confusion and reduce confidence in finding destinations. For administrators: treat wayfinding as a patient-experience metric, not only a facilities task.

Patients’ Perception of Signage Systems →

2. Public-hospital wayfinding signage has measurable gaps

Research on wayfinding signage in public hospitals documents how layout complexity, language, and placement affect whether visitors reach OPD, labs, and wards on time. For administrators: audit routes from gate to highest-volume destinations before adding more boards.

Wayfinding Signage in Public Hospitals →

3. Patients and visitors have distinct navigational needs

Work on the navigational needs of hospital patients and visitors shows that first-time attendants, elderly patients, and people with limited literacy struggle more with jargon-heavy maps. For administrators: prefer plain-language destinations and local-language directions over English-only directories.

Navigational Needs of Hospital Patients and Visitors →

4. Digital wayfinding complements — and updates faster than — signage

Industry and clinical communications writing on digital wayfinding in hospitals argues that digital maps reduce desk interruptions and stay accurate when departments move. For administrators: pair physical signs with a QR-based route that updates without a campus reprint.

Why digital wayfinding helps hospital communications →
