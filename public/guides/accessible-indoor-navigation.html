Guide · Accessibility

Accessible indoor navigation and PwD-friendly routes

Inclusive wayfinding is more than an icon on a map. It is route choice, language, and honest information about lifts and barriers.

Many hospitals publish a wheelchair symbol near the entrance and assume the job is done. Visitors who use wheelchairs, walkers, or who cannot climb stairs still get sent up staircases by default “shortest path” thinking. Attendants carrying patients on stretchers face the same trap. Accessible indoor navigation means the digital route prefers paths that the person can actually use.

What accessibility means for indoor routes

  • Step-free paths — avoid stairs when a lift or ramp alternative exists.
  • Lift-aware guidance — tell people which lift bank serves which floors, and when a lift is the required link.
  • Honest gaps — if a destination has no step-free access, say so early rather than stranding someone mid-route.
  • Readable language — plain destination names help people with cognitive load, low literacy, or stress.
  • Rest and distance cues — approximate time and distance help people who fatigue quickly.

Building survey habits that include accessibility

When mapping a facility, treat accessibility as first-class data, not a filter bolted on later. Surveyors should note stair-only connectors, lift locations, ramp gradients where relevant, and doors that are routinely locked. Facilities teams should confirm which lifts are public versus staff-only. A beautiful map that routes wheelchair users through a staff stairwell is worse than no map.

Policy and trust signals

Indian hospitals increasingly treat inclusion as part of patient experience and compliance culture. BootWay’s routing practices have been validated in accessibility training contexts; see our PwD accessibility validation alongside ISO 27001 and DPIIT recognition. Certification does not replace a site-specific survey — every building is different — but it signals that inclusive routing is part of the product design, not an afterthought.

Operational tips for administrators

  1. Offer an explicit “accessible route” choice in the visitor UI, not only a hidden default.
  2. Align digital routes with physical accessible signage and lift directories.
  3. Train security not to override accessible routes with “faster” stair shortcuts.
  4. Re-check routes after renovations — accessibility breaks silently when a ramp is blocked.
  5. Collect feedback from disability advocacy groups or patient advisory panels after pilot.

How this ties to BootWay

BootWay’s indoor navigation supports accessible routing options such as step-free and lift-aware paths where the building allows. Combined with multilingual directions and QR entry without an app, the goal is that more visitors can complete the journey independently.
